Gridline C: The Base Plates Won’t Drop

Optional Week — When to Run It

The Quality module is now two weeks by default (Week 2 and Week 3) with one optional third session. Run Week 3B only if the cohort has asked for more time or is behind on the Quality e-Learning and quiz — it is the coach’s call, based on the cohort’s pace. If the cohort is on pace, skip it and go straight to Week 4 (Safety); nothing later in the program depends on it.

It sits after Week 3 (Quality Wrap-Up) as the final Quality session. Weeks 4–12 keep their numbers whether or not it runs.

Learning Objectives
  • Apply the four Quality concepts from the NCCER course — the Quality System, Delivering Quality, Inspections and Testing, and Quality Culture — to a realistic structural steel decision made under schedule pressure.
  • Decide when to stop work, who to notify, and what has to be documented before work continues (hold points, RFIs, written direction).
  • Recognize how a foreman’s reaction shapes whether crew members speak up.
  • Leave with one specific habit to use on the next job.
Before the Session — Facilitator Prep
  • Read the scenario and all three rounds ahead of time. Decide whether to run the whole group together (smaller cohorts) or in Teams breakout rooms of 3–4 foremen (8 or more).
  • Hold each complication back until its round — the surprise is the point. Put each one on a slide or paste it into the Teams chat at the moment you need it.
  • Keep a visible timer. Each round is short on purpose; the facilitator’s main job is keeping it moving.
  • Have the Microsoft Form link ready to paste into the chat for the last five minutes (link to be added once the coach has built the Form).
  • Have one real example of your own ready in case the group is quiet. After every round, ask: “Has anyone seen this on their job?” — that keeps the activity tied to real field experience, the same way Weeks 2 and 3 are.
  • No pre-work is needed beyond the Quality e-Learning.

The Scenario

You are the foreman for a six-person steel erection crew on a four-story, 58,000-square-foot medical office building. It is Monday, 6:45 a.m. A 90-ton crane is on site, rented through Friday, and the first delivery of columns and beams is on the ground and sorted. The first pick is scheduled for 7:00.

Last week the concrete subcontractor poured the piers and set the anchor rods. You have not received a surveyed as-built of the anchor rods yet — the GC said it would “come today.” While your connector lays out base plates at the first twelve columns, he tells you the plate will not drop over the rods on four of them. On two of those columns, a rod is off by about an inch and a half. On one, a rod is visibly bent over, as if something hit it.

Key facts: twelve columns are planned for the first day; four of the twelve are affected; the crane is costing thousands of dollars a day; the owner’s special inspector is scheduled for first-work inspection on Tuesday; the drawings call for four anchor rods at each column.

Round 1

“Stop or Go?”

7 min

Verify before you build on top of it. Hold each complication until you’re ready for it.

Facilitator steps
  • Read the scenario. Have everyone type their first move in the chat (about 60 seconds).
  • Read the complication below out loud.
  • Call on two or three foremen for each question. Push for specifics — “Measure what, against what?”
Complication

The superintendent calls: “I’ve got a crane burning money. Just burn the holes out a little — slot ’em — and set the columns. We’ll get the engineer to sign off later. We’ve done it a hundred times.” The concrete foreman standing next to you adds: “That’s within normal. I’ve seen worse.”

Discussion questions

  1. Q1Before the first pick, what do you do — and what do you measure against?
  2. Q2Who needs to hear about this, and in what order?
  3. Q3What is actually wrong with “fix it now, paper it later”?
  4. Q4How do you keep the crew productive while this gets sorted out?
Suggested responses (facilitator only)

Strong answers:

  • Hold the four affected columns and let no one touch the rods or the plates. Verify instead of assuming: measure the rod groups against the anchor rod plan and the column lines, and check the tolerance in the contract documents. For reference, the AISC Code of Standard Practice allows about 1/8 inch within a rod group and 1/4 inch between adjacent groups — an inch and a half is far outside that.
  • Check the eight “good” columns too. If four are wrong, trust none of them until they are measured.
  • Take photos with a tape in the frame, mark the rods, and write down what was found and when.
  • Notify the GC superintendent and your own PM right away with facts, not blame, and ask for the engineer of record’s direction in writing (an RFI). Ask for the as-built survey today.
  • Give the super a path instead of a flat no: “I’m not saying no. I need the engineer’s yes in writing, and in the meantime I’ll keep the crane busy.”
  • Keep the crew working: set the columns that check out, sort and stage steel for the next area, pre-assemble, and check bolts and materials.

Why it matters: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.755(b)(1) says anchor rods (anchor bolts) shall not be repaired, replaced, or field-modified without the approval of the project structural engineer of record. Base plates and rods are engineered to carry load into the foundation; a field fix changes how that load travels, and the crew cannot see the difference.

Red flags: torching or slotting the plate holes; heating or bending a rod; “we’ll fix it later”; going quiet; blaming the concrete crew in front of your crew; setting all twelve anyway.

Concepts: Quality System (hold points and the RFI process) and Inspections and Testing (verify before you build on top of it).

Round 2

“The Quiet Crew Member”

7 min

Slow down on Complication B. This is where the culture conversation lives.

Facilitator steps
  • Read Complication A first (it is urgent), then Complication B.
  • Slow down on Complication B — this is where the culture conversation lives. Let the group sit in it for a few seconds.
  • Ask for at least one answer from someone who has been the quiet crew member themselves.
Complication A

Across the pad, your most experienced journeyman has fired up a rosebud torch and is heating the bent rod to straighten it. “Saves us a day, boss. We do this all the time.”

Complication B

During the stand-down, your newest apprentice pulls you aside: “I noticed those rods looked off on Friday when I was helping with layout. I figured somebody already checked. Last month the super told me to quit asking questions and just do my job, so I didn’t say anything.”

Discussion questions

  1. Q5Complication A: what do you do in the next 30 seconds? What do you say to him later?
  2. Q6Complication B: what do you say to the apprentice? What do you NOT say?
  3. Q7What would have made it easy for him to speak up on Friday? What does your crew learn from how you handle both people today?
  4. Q8Is this a person problem or a system problem? What system change would fix it?
Suggested responses (facilitator only)

Strong answer to A: stop it calmly and immediately (“Hold up — leave that rod alone”) without shouting or embarrassing him in front of the crew. Photograph and tag the rod, and tell the engineer and the super that it was heated — the engineer needs to know the rod’s real condition. Do not set a column on it. Later, in private, explain why: heat can change the steel in ways nobody can see, and only the engineer can approve a modification. Acknowledge that he was trying to help the schedule.

Strong answer to B: thank him directly, and in front of the crew if he is comfortable with that: “That’s exactly what I need from you.” Be honest that Friday would have been better, and make the next time easy. Own the super issue yourself — “I’ll handle the super.” Don’t say “why didn’t you tell me?” in an accusing tone, and don’t promise something you can’t deliver.

System fixes: add a 60-second “what are you seeing that doesn’t look right?” to every pre-task talk; make it explicit that anyone can stop work for a quality or safety concern; the foreman checks foundations and anchor rods before steel is delivered; recognize catches out loud.

Red flags: ignoring the torch because the schedule is tight; yelling; “next time just tell me” with nothing changed; treating it as the apprentice’s fault.

Concepts: Quality Culture, and Delivering Quality — “we always do it that way” is not a standard.

Round 3

“Closing the Loop”

6 min

Close the loop: records, hold points, and getting the schedule back honestly.

Facilitator steps
  • Read Complication C, run Questions 9 and 10 against it, then read Complication D and finish with Questions 11 and 12.
  • If time is short, drop Question 11 — see the time-cut note at the end.
Complication C

Mid-afternoon, the engineer’s written response comes back. Two columns can use oversized holes with plate washers. One base plate has to be re-detailed and refabricated. The bent rod must be replaced with an adhesive anchor that the special inspector has to witness. Total: about two days.

Complication D

The next morning the super says: “We lost a day. Set the remaining columns this afternoon. The special inspector won’t be here until Wednesday and we can’t wait on him.” Then the GC’s QC manager walks up and asks: “Show me your paper trail on Gridline C.”

Discussion questions

  1. Q9By end of day Monday, what records should exist? What would you hand the QC manager?
  2. Q10The inspector can’t come until Wednesday. Do you set the columns? Why or why not?
  3. Q11How do you win back the schedule without cutting quality?
  4. Q12What will you do at the start of your next job so this doesn’t happen to you again?
Suggested responses (facilitator only)

Records: dated photos with a tape in the frame; measurements; when the problem was found and who was notified and when; the RFI with sketch and photos; the engineer’s written response and any revised drawings; tags on the affected columns; and a daily report entry that includes the heated rod. Nobody should have to reconstruct this from memory.

The inspector question: check the quality plan and the specs — is the first-work inspection a hold point? If it is, you don’t build on uninspected work. If anyone wants to proceed “at risk,” that needs the GC’s or owner’s approval in writing. Don’t repeat the same condition across many columns before the first one is accepted — one failed inspection after twenty columns is twenty columns of rework. Ask for an earlier inspection slot instead.

Winning back time: resequence to unaffected areas, add crew or crane time where the work is clear, and sit down with the PM and super on a recovery plan. Trade effort for time — never trade approvals or inspections for time. Document the cause (the foundation condition) with facts in your daily reports; the PM handles any time or cost claims.

Next job: verify anchor rod layout against the plan and the tolerances before the crane shows up; get the as-built survey in hand first; walk the foundations; make “foundation check” a hold point on your pre-erection checklist; raise it at the preconstruction meeting.

Red flags: setting the columns “at risk” with no written approval; “we’ll note it on the daily”; leaving the heated rod out of the paperwork; writing “no issues” in the daily report.

Concepts: Quality System (documentation and closing the RFI loop), Inspections and Testing (hold points), Delivering Quality, and Quality Culture.

Debrief and Commit

Ask the group: “Which Quality concept broke first?” The honest answer is all four — the Quality System had no verification of the foundation before steel arrived; the crew reached for the way it’s always been done instead of the standard; nothing was inspected before work was built on top of it; and a crew member stayed quiet. Then use the same three questions as Weeks 2 and 3: Where should this have been prevented or caught? What is the foreman’s role? What action would keep it from happening again?

Finish by asking each foreman to type one sentence: “Starting this week, I will ___.” Then move straight into the Form.

    Notes for facilitators

    This week is optional and based on the cohort’s pace. It was added on 2026-10-09 at the cohort’s request for more time on the Quality module. It has no old-week equivalent in the original 18-week program.

    The scenario is fictional on purpose, but it is built from conditions common on steel jobs: out-of-tolerance anchor rods, schedule pressure from an idling crane, informal field fixes, and a crew member who does not speak up. Use it alongside real experience, not instead of it — ask “has anyone seen this?” after every round.

    References used: OSHA 29 CFR 1926.755(b)(1) (no repair, replacement, or field modification of anchor rods without the structural engineer of record’s approval) and the AISC Code of Standard Practice anchor rod placement tolerances (about 1/8 inch within a group, 1/4 inch between groups). The scenario illustrates a decision process, not an engineering ruling — the project specs and the engineer of record always govern, so check them for your own project before quoting numbers.

    If time runs short, drop Question 11 (win back the schedule) and one wildcard first. Protect Round 2 — the culture conversation is the heart of the activity.

    Do not present the suggested responses as the only right answers. If a foreman’s approach is different but sound, ask them to explain it to the group.
